This is the Korea Daily Brief from the CSIS Korea Chair, read by an AI voice. It's Tuesday, November 10.

Three things today: North Korea tests a new torpedo and sets a 2027 deployment deadline, Seoul's Cabinet approves expanding the Cheonghae anti-piracy unit toward the Strait of Hormuz, and President Lee Jae-myung calls for bipartisan outreach to the new Democratic Congress. The torpedo test is North Korea's second in a month, and it comes with a named deployment date, which is a different kind of signal.

The National Assembly is expected to hold a plenary session on November 12 to process several key bills.

First, North Korea's second torpedo test in a month.

On Monday, North Korea's Academy of Defense Sciences, Missile Administration, and Torpedo Weapon Research Bureau jointly tested what state media called a "new-type high-speed torpedo weapon system." That was the description in the official KCNA announcement. And the announcement did something the first test report, disclosed after an October 15 test, also did: it named a deployment date. The Navy will be equipped with the weapon from the beginning of 2027.

What's different this time is what's missing. KCNA released no photographs and fewer technical details than the initial disclosure. That restraint may matter to analysts trying to assess the system's capabilities, but the deployment timeline is the headline.

The torpedo test isn't the only North Korean military signal this week. Kim Yo Jong, the vice department director of the Workers' Party of Korea's Central Committee, issued a sharp public statement rejecting a call by U.N. Secretary-General Antonio Guterres for renewed inter-Korean dialogue. Guterres had made the appeal following a Demilitarized Zone landmine incident. Kim accused him of taking a "prejudiced and irresponsible attitude" and of "echoing South Korea's unilateral and provocative sophism." She warned that if what she called "reckless military provocations of South Korea gangsters lead to an undesirable incident in the southern border area," the Secretary-General would bear responsibility. Reuters also carried the statement.

Watch for any South Korean or U.S. response to either.

Second, Seoul moves the Cheonghae unit toward the Strait of Hormuz.

South Korea's Cabinet has approved a motion to expand the Cheonghae anti-piracy unit's deployment zone toward the Hormuz area, and the motion now goes to the National Assembly, where the ruling Democratic Party holds a majority. The expanded mandate also adds "support for the restoration of freedom of navigation" to the unit's mission.

Iran's Foreign Ministry urged Seoul on Monday to refrain from deployment to the Hormuz area. Seoul reiterated what it called its "non-involvement, non-intervention" principle.

The diplomatic groundwork has been building in parallel. Foreign Minister Cho Hyun, on the sidelines of the second G7 Foreign Ministers' Meeting in Paris, met with Saudi Foreign Minister Faisal bin Farhan Al Saud on November 9. In a post following that meeting, Cho said both sides agreed that "restoring freedom of navigation based on international law in the Strait of Hormuz is important." Ministers also agreed to expand cooperation in nuclear energy, advanced technology, and defense.

Cho also held bilaterals with French Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot and Egyptian Foreign Minister Badr Abdelatty at the same gathering. With France, ministers reviewed the upgrade of bilateral ties to "global strategic partner" status on the 140th anniversary of diplomatic relations.

Assembly consent is the next gate. Watch whether the Democratic majority moves quickly or conditions its approval.

Third, President Lee on the new Democratic Congress.

President Lee Jae-myung used a Cabinet meeting to frame the Democratic Party's seizure of both chambers of Congress in last week's U.S. midterm elections as a moment requiring active Korean management, not passive observation. Lee said South Korea must maintain close cooperation with the U.S. administration while "establishing a bipartisan foundation for cooperation in Congress." He instructed officials to prepare scenario-based responses to external uncertainties and to minimize negative effects on the domestic economy.

Lee's instructions come with some concrete encouragement. Pro-Korea Democratic lawmakers are emerging as frontrunners to chair key House and Senate foreign affairs and security committees, according to Paijinancial News.

CSIS Korea Chair Victor Cha and colleagues addressed the same dynamics in a press briefing on President Trump's upcoming Asia trip. They flagged the ASEAN stop in Manila as more substantively significant than the APEC meetings, and noted that the Democratic midterm sweep changes the Congress variable for the alliance's remaining two Trump years, particularly on wartime operational control transfer conditions and security cooperation.

The Diplomat published an analysis arguing that durable U.S.-Korea policy requires bipartisan alignment that transcends which party controls the White House or Congress, and that Democrats gaining leverage over joint exercises and summit concessions is an opportunity to anchor that framework.

The key fact to hold: Lee wants a bipartisan congressional strategy, and the committee chairs who will shape that relationship are still being determined.

Now to Pyongyang.

State media's main domestic story today has Kim Jong Un stressing the importance of agriculture and vowing to improve living conditions in rural areas, continuing a welfare and domestic-messaging theme that has led KCNA for several days. The torpedo test was confirmed in state media, but the weapons content is absent from internal North Korea news. Kim Yo Jong's statement attacking U.N. Secretary-General Guterres over the DMZ landmine dispute was the other major output. Kim Jong Un has not made a public appearance in two days. The bottom line from state media: the torpedo program is being announced outward, while the domestic audience keeps hearing about farms.

The rest of the news, quickly.

On personnel, President Lee nominated Lee Yong-cheol, currently director of the Defense Acquisition Program Administration, as justice minister nominee. His confirmation hearing preparation team begins work November 11. Media scrutiny has focused on his anticipated stance on prosecution withdrawal of pending cases.

Defense Minister Kang Shin-chul reiterated his push to consolidate South Korea's three service academies into a single institution. Kang met with cadets from the Army, Navy, and Air Force academies at the Korea Military Academy and said the proposed National Military Academy's location remains under discussion.

South Korea's Ministry of Unification confirmed it has custody of a drifting North Korean fishing vessel taken on November 3. The ministry has not disclosed the crew size or whether anyone aboard has expressed intent to defect.

The United States has blacklisted a Seychelles-based cryptocurrency exchange over North Korean money laundering, according to N.K. News.

A Pacific Forum analysis identifies three unclosed gaps in South Korea's wartime operational control certification: command authority, network rebuilding, which the analysis says cannot be completed before 2029, and an undefined cyber chain of command. The annual Security Consultative Meeting has slipped to early 2027.

North Korea moved top artificial intelligence specialists from its spy agency, the Reconnaissance Information General Bureau, to the Foreign Ministry beginning in late October, according to Daily N.K.. The transfers have stirred resentment inside the intelligence unit.

Former deputy secretary of state Kurt Campbell said North Korea has communicated that U.S. recognition of its nuclear status is a precondition for a summit, complicating Trump's Asia-trip diplomacy.

South Korea's Financial Security Institute was hacked. The breach was detected November 9, and the institute notified the Financial Services Commission. Financial firms' security data was leaked.

The International Atomic Energy Agency's Director General Grossi met with South Korean Vice Foreign Minister Jeong Yeon-doo and welcomed Seoul's safeguards commitment on nuclear submarines.

A Gallup Korea poll taken October 27 to 29 put President Lee's approval at 39 percent. The Democratic Party stood at 35 percent, and the People Power Party at 30 percent.

On Capitol Hill.

Analysis from Businesskorea finds that Democratic majorities are likely to pursue stricter data-privacy, climate, and supply-chain regulations that could raise compliance costs for South Korean exporters, even as Democrats are seen as more receptive on alliance matters. Separately, Representative Young Kim, a Korean-American Republican who co-chaired the Congressional Caucus on Korea, lost her reelection bid, removing a prominent bipartisan Korea voice from the House.

Beyond the region.

South Korea plans to announce a 100 million dollar Ukraine aid package. That commitment aligns with what Foreign Minister Cho Hyun discussed with French Foreign Minister Barrot in Paris, where ministers also reviewed Seoul's planned aid. Poland received a new delivery of Korean-made K2 main battle tanks and K9 self-propelled howitzers, continuing deliveries under a 2022 framework arms deal. Poland is among the largest customers of South Korean defense hardware on NATO's eastern flank.

Finally, the markets.

Seoul's main stock index, the KOSPI, surged about 4.4 percent to close at about 5,239, led by chipmakers Samsung and SK Hynix on dip buying after the previous session's selloff. The won strengthened, with the dollar buying about 1,339 won. Brent crude fell about 2 percent to around 90 dollars.
