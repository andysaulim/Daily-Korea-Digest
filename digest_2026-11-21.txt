Three things today: North Korea's uranium enrichment plant at Yongbyon is getting bigger. South Korea's Democrats set a date for a Supreme Court impeachment push. And Kim Jong Un tours factories while Trump's bid for a meeting goes unanswered.

That Yongbyon expansion is the story to watch. The International Atomic Energy Agency says North Korea has finished construction work on a new enrichment facility extension, and satellite photos back that up. Tomorrow, Mongolia's foreign minister arrives in Seoul for a four-day visit.

First, the Yongbyon uranium-enrichment plant is growing, and international monitors are alarmed.

The Wall Street Journal published new satellite photographs Saturday showing clear expansion of the enrichment facility at Yongbyon. The same day, the International Atomic Energy Agency confirmed that North Korea has completed external construction work on an enrichment facility extension. The agency voiced serious concern over the upgrades.

That finding is corroborated by Reuters, AP, and France 24. A tracker monitoring the site shows a new centrifuge hall has been operational since April 2026, with thermal signatures consistent with increased production.

For Korea policy, this matters because it means North Korea's ability to produce enriched uranium, the material used in nuclear weapons, is expanding in a measurable, confirmed way, right as diplomacy stalls.

That stall is on full display this week. Trump departed Asia after the APEC summit in Shenzhen without meeting Kim Jong Un, saying the encounter did not "work out" but that it would happen "at some point." Pyongyang has said nothing. Trump did tell reporters that a U.S.-China-Russia trilateral at APEC discussed dangers on the Korean Peninsula. CSIS experts, in a new assessment of Trump's Asia trip, note that the absence of a Trump-Kim meeting and the nuclear discussion at the trilateral are the primary Korea takeaways from the summit.

The Korea Economic Institute of America also published analysis arguing Kim's October visit to China deserves closer scrutiny, given its implications for North Korea's alignment with Beijing and Moscow.

What to watch: Yongbyon's centrifuge hall is now confirmed operational. The enrichment expansion, the failed Trump-Kim meeting, and the trilateral nuclear discussion are three signals pointing in the same direction.

Second, South Korea's Democratic Party has picked a date and a target for its next impeachment push.

The party formally adopted the impeachment of Supreme Court Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae as party policy on November 20. Representative Yang Bu-nam has been named to lead a task force on the effort. SBS reports the party has set December 3 as the target date to introduce the motion. That date marks the second anniversary of the martial law declaration.

The numbers make this straightforward for the Democrats. The constitution requires at least 100 members to introduce an impeachment motion and passage by 150 members. The Democratic Party holds 161 seats, meaning it can do both without a single vote from the opposition.

The People Power Party announced it will defend Chief Justice Cho. The party held a rally in Daegu, claiming 100,000 participants turned out.

Watch the calendar. December 3 is the trigger date. The National Assembly has already been moving. This week it passed the Democracy Patriots Act, providing state honors and benefits to victims and contributors to South Korea's democratization movement, over People Power Party opposition.

Meanwhile, Unification Minister Chung Dong-young held a rare meeting with U.S. Ambassador Steel and U.S. Forces Korea Commander General Xavier Brunson at Panmunjom. Korean media described the venue and format as unusual. It was the first encounter between Chung and the ambassador after a 113-day gap.

The December 3 date is now on the calendar. That's the day Democrats say they'll move on Chief Justice Cho.

Third, Kim Jong Un was visible Saturday, but not in the way Trump was hoping.

North Korean state media released images showing Kim inspecting teaching aids and science class equipment at factory sites. The Korean Central News Agency said he examined samples of school fixtures, in its words, "of all indices." It was a firmly domestic image, the leader focused on education, with no reference to foreign policy.

That silence is the story. Trump had sought a meeting at APEC in Shenzhen. It didn't happen. And Pyongyang has not issued a single word in response to Trump's public remarks about wanting to meet. State media spent the APEC week focused on North Korea's diplomatic ties with allied states, and that pattern continued Saturday.

KCNA also ran rhetoric about adversary naval activities, with Kim described as slamming what state media called enemy naval operations. Separately, N.K. News reports that a petty theft case at a Russian drone facility has exposed the deployment of a North Korean worker at a key Russian drone site, another data point in the documented presence of North Korean workers in Russia's defense industry.

North Korea and Laos also vowed deeper cooperation, with the North Korean premier meeting a senior Lao delegation in Pyongyang.

The bottom line: Kim was on camera Saturday, but on his terms. The educational factory visit was the dominant image of the day. Silence on Trump's outreach remains Pyongyang's answer.

Now to Pyongyang.

The leading story in North Korean state media this week, as reported by N.K. News, is the pattern of silence itself. KCNA produced no output acknowledging Trump's remarks about wanting to meet Kim during the Shenzhen APEC summit. Instead, state media emphasized North Korea's ties with allied states throughout the week.

Kim's appearance today at educational factory sites, examining school fixtures and science class equipment, is what state media offered as the domestic image of the day. He appeared one day after his previous public appearance.

The bottom line from the KCNA picture: Pyongyang is projecting normalcy at home and solidarity with partners abroad, while saying nothing about the failed Trump meeting or the APEC trilateral discussion of Korean Peninsula dangers.

The rest of the news, quickly.

President Lee Jae-myung visited an SK artificial intelligence data center in Ulsan on Saturday. Speaking at the site, Lee said, "When a world is under a heated race, speed is crucial." He called for government attention to construction-worker shortages at advanced facilities. Lee has now visited the three regional mega-project zones.

Also from the president's office: Lee clarified the government's reward for reporting College Scholastic Ability Test cheating. The reward is at least 100 million won per case, not a maximum, and multiple reports multiply the payout. Lee called exam cheating "a crime as serious as corruption by public officials." The Korea Times reports the cheating crisis has shaken public confidence in South Korea's meritocracy system.

A freight train derailed near Sejong early Saturday morning, with five cars going off the tracks at Sojeong-ri at five thirty-seven a.m. The Gyeongbu line was suspended but is expected to resume Sunday. High-speed KTX service was not affected.

Switzerland cited the camp's role in transfers of Ukrainian children.

A Japanese government survey finds 62 percent of Japanese respondents feel friendly toward South Korea, the highest level on record, as Japan-Korea ties have warmed. Affinity toward China remained low.

On trade, South Korea posted a 43.3 billion dollar surplus with China in the January-to-October period, the first surplus in four years. But the surplus is entirely driven by semiconductor exports. Excluding semiconductors, the deficit with China widened by 12.4 billion dollars year-on-year, with autos, batteries, and electric equipment all deteriorating.

In corporate news, Korea Zinc rejected pressure from activist shareholders MBK Partners and Youngpoong, demanding they account for the collapse of Homeplus before questioning Korea Zinc's governance. A shareholder vote is approaching.

Gasoline prices in South Korea fell for the 27th consecutive week. The national average dropped to 1,854 won per liter in the third week of November, with Seoul entering the 1,800-won range.

Gallup Korea's poll, taken November 3 to 5, shows President Lee's approval at 41 percent, up from the previous reading. The Democratic Party stands at 36 percent, also up. The People Power Party is at 28 percent, down. Independents are also at 28 percent, stable.

Looking ahead: Mongolia's Foreign Minister Battsetseg begins a four-day official visit to Seoul tomorrow. Justice Minister nominee Lee Yong-cheol faces a National Assembly confirmation hearing on November 24.

On Capitol Hill.

The Korea Economic Institute of America argues Seoul should engage the new Congress before positions harden. The analysis points to the 2018 midterm precedent, when Democrats capped U.S. Forces Korea troop levels at 28,500. With Democrats now having taken both chambers in the 2026 midterms, the analysis says new alliance defenders have arrived, but that the window for shaping their views is open now.

Beyond the region.

South Korea's defense industry is deepening its Middle East engagement, moving beyond equipment sales into production and technology cooperation arrangements. Seoul describes the shift as part of a broader effort to diversify its defense partnerships.

South Korea is also pursuing contracts for two Thai railway lines and a Bangkok Port revamp, expanding its infrastructure export push in Southeast Asia. No contract values or timelines have been reported.

Finally, the markets.

Seoul's main stock index, the KOSPI, fell about 5.2 percent on November 20, closing at about 4,870. The won traded at about 1,335 to the dollar. The tech selloff was global: the Nasdaq fell about 5.3 percent the same day, erasing all of its 2026 gains, with semiconductor stocks leading the decline. Korean retail investors who bought into Nvidia during October are sitting on significant unrealized losses.
