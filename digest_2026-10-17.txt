This is the Korea Daily Brief from the CSIS Korea Chair. It's Saturday, October 17.

Pyongyang says its new torpedo can think for itself. Three things today: North Korea's AI torpedo and what state media is saying about it, a sovereignty dispute between South Korean lawmakers and the top American commander on the peninsula, and Japan's prime minister makes a shrine offering while promising to honor a landmark historical declaration.

North Korea tested an underwater torpedo on Thursday. That much KCNA confirmed Saturday. But what made the announcement stand out is what Pyongyang said is inside it. State media described the weapon, designated the Zam-2.0, as a high-speed precision-guided munition equipped with artificial intelligence — the first time North Korea has publicly named a weapon with AI targeting capability. KCNA called it a major naval breakthrough.

The test is the sixth North Korea weapons event this month. It follows a submarine-launched cruise missile test confirmed earlier this week on October 15 and 16. That's a sustained testing surge that has run through all of October. No images accompanied the announcement. And Kim Jong Un has not been publicly associated with this test. In fact, Saturday marks four consecutive days without a confirmed Kim appearance.

There's one more layer from state media. A North Korean official identified as the missile sector chief used a separate KCNA statement to publicly accuse South Korea of fabricating its own hypersonic glide vehicle test, and cited that accusation as justification for North Korea's continued arms buildup. That's Pyongyang challenging Seoul's deterrence claims in public, in the same week it announced its sixth weapons test. Watch for Kim's next confirmed appearance, and watch October 19th — two consecutive years, 2021 and 2022, saw North Korea provocations on that date.

The bottom line: a North Korean torpedo publicly designated with AI targeting, the sixth weapons event in October, and Kim Jong Un out of public view for four days running.

At an October 14 event hosted by a strategic studies institute, USFK Commander General Xavier Brunson reportedly said, quote, "the Republic of Korea starts at the southern fence."

Democratic Party members of the National Assembly Defense Committee are demanding he retract those words and apologize. DP defense committee whip Rep. Kim Byung-joo posted Saturday that the armistice grants the UN Command authority only over DMZ access and civil administration — not over South Korea territorial sovereignty. Kim called the statement a, quote, "line-crossing remark." Defense Committee Chairman Rep. Jin Seong-jun had flagged the same remarks the day before, saying the statement, quote, "cannot help but raise serious misunderstandings about the areas where South Korea sovereignty applies."

The political pressure is now coming from both the committee chair and the DP's defense whip simultaneously. That amplifies the call on both USFK and South Korea's Ministry of National Defense to respond. The episode is unfolding against the backdrop of an ongoing debate over operational control transfer — a question that makes any perceived ambiguity about where Korean sovereignty begins especially sensitive.

The key fact: Democratic Party lawmakers say General Brunson's "southern fence" remark denies South Korea sovereignty and are publicly demanding a retraction and an apology.

Japan Prime Minister Takaichi Sanae sent a ritual offering — a masakaki — to Yasukuni Shrine on Saturday. She did not make a personal visit. South Korea's government responded with a formal statement expressing, in its words, "deep disappointment and regret," calling on Tokyo to reflect on history with humility. The statement follows Seoul's standard diplomatic formula for Yasukuni incidents, stopping short of a formal protest or summoning the ambassador.

At the same time, Takaichi told Japan's upper house budget committee that she will uphold the 1995 Murayama Declaration. That's a significant rhetorical shift. She had criticized the declaration on the record in 2005 and 2013. Saturday she said, quote, "the position of past cabinets will remain unchanged," and that, quote, "the Murayama Declaration is of course included." When asked whether her views had changed, Takaichi said, quote, "I am growing every day."

That combination — a shrine offering and a Murayama reaffirmation on the same day — tracks a pattern seen with LDP leaders managing domestic nationalist constituencies while trying to limit diplomatic fallout with Seoul. What to watch: whether Seoul's response stays at the level of a statement, or whether the political temperature rises enough to trigger a stronger diplomatic move.

The key fact: Takaichi sent a Yasukuni offering, Seoul expressed deep disappointment, and the same day she pledged to uphold the Murayama Declaration.

State media confirmed all three of today's major stories in some form. On the torpedo: KCNA reported Saturday that the Zam-2.0 test took place Thursday, called it a major naval breakthrough, and released no images — a notable absence for a weapon of this claimed significance. On the New Yalu River Bridge connecting Sinuiju with Dandong, China: KCNA confirmed an opening ceremony was held — the first official North Korean acknowledgment that the bridge is operational, more than a decade after construction was completed. And separately, the unnamed North Korean missile sector chief accused South Korea of fabricating its hypersonic glide vehicle test, using the accusation to justify Pyongyang's arms buildup.

Kim Jong Un did not appear in any of Saturday's KCNA reporting. The brief's bottom line: watch for his next confirmed appearance, and watch for any KCNA framing of signals from Washington about a potential Trump-Kim meeting.

A few other stories worth your attention this morning.

On North Korea's expanding diplomatic footprint: Belarus announced it opened an embassy in Pyongyang this month — the first permanent mission in North Korea, coming nearly three months behind schedule. Belarus Foreign Minister Maxim Ryzhenkov had visited Pyongyang in August to meet Foreign Minister Choe Son Hui, and the two sides discussed mutual visa-free arrangements. A follow-up meeting at the UN General Assembly in September focused specifically on the embassy question. Minsk framed the openings — it opened a simultaneous mission in Myanmar — as part of a deliberate policy to strengthen presence among what it called countries on "the axis of distant diplomacy." North Korean state media had not reported the opening as of Saturday morning.

The Financial Times frames North Korea's dual bridge openings — one toward China, one toward Russia — as deliberate parallel hedging toward both Beijing and Moscow. And a Nikkei analysis argues North Korea-Russia treaty framework is designed as a long-term structural realignment, not a wartime arrangement tied to Ukraine.

On analysis: Former NSC officials Andrea Kendall-Taylor and Richard Fontaine argue in Foreign Affairs that Russia, China, Iran, and North Korea have moved from rhetorical solidarity to operational coordination — sharing weapons, technology, and diplomatic cover — while U.S. alliance management is weakening the counter-network. The piece is directly relevant to any debate over whether a Trump-Kim diplomatic track can be separated from North Korea's weapons flows to Russia.

In South Korea: President Lee Jae-myung visited the funeral home of firefighter Kim Jin-seok, who died early Saturday after suffering cardiac arrest at an October 16 factory fire in Bucheon. Lee knelt to offer incense, posthumously awarded Kim the Okjo Geunjeong Medal, and wrote in the condolence book — quote — "In the safe daily lives you protected, the people will forever remember your sacrifice." Democratic Party Chairman Kim Min-seok also apologized Saturday for Gyeonggi Governor Choo Mi-ae's earlier comments about the fire service.

On the economy: Bank of Korea Governor Shin Hyun-song told the G30 International Banking Seminar in Bangkok Saturday that the Bank of Korea is piloting a new system called Bank of Korea International Wire — allowing non-Korean banks to hold won accounts and send won-denominated remittances globally. Shin called now "the right moment" for full liberalization, with a full launch expected by January.

In technology: the volume of deepfake sex crime videos submitted for review has already surpassed the full-year 2025 figure, eight months into 2026, intensifying pressure on the National Assembly to tighten digital sex crime law.

In the polls: Gallup Korea's October 13-to-15 survey puts President Lee's approval at 39 percent, up two points. The Democratic Party is at 36 percent, the People Power Party at 27 percent, and independents at 29 percent, down.

On your calendar: October 19th is worth watching. The CSIS North Korea Provocations Database shows provocations on that date in both 2021 and 2022. The current October testing surge raises the watch posture.

Markets. Seoul's main stock index closed Thursday at about 5,668, up just over 2 percent. The won is trading at about 1,330 to the dollar, stronger by less than 1 percent. Brent crude has climbed sharply — up about 4 percent to just under 110 dollars a barrel. The Bank of Korea's policy rate stands at 2.5 percent.
