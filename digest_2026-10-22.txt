This is the Korea Daily Brief from the CSIS Korea Chair, read by an AI voice. It's Thursday, October 22.

Three things today: Kim Yo Jong sets a loudspeaker deadline at the border, the Bank of Korea holds rates steady, and Seoul takes the DMZ mine blast to the U.N. Security Council. On the loudspeaker story: North Korea is telling border troops to use force if threatened, while Seoul is still deciding how to respond. Tomorrow marks one year since Kim Jong Un honored North Korean soldiers who fought in Russia.

First, the border is getting louder, and potentially more dangerous.

Kim Yo Jong, Vice Department Director of the Korean Workers' Party, declared in a statement through the Korean Central News Agency that North Korea's border loudspeakers will be operational within a few days. She made that announcement after inspecting frontline fortification sites alongside military marshal Pak Jong Chon of the Korean People's Army. That inspection took place on October 21.

She didn't stop there. Kim also personally demanded that a South Korean presidential adviser, whom she criticized by name, quote, "stop rambling and shut his mouth," after he criticized Pyongyang's loudspeaker plan.

Alongside the loudspeaker threat, North Korea ordered its border troops to take what it called resolute action if South Korea threatens their safety. That force authorization, reported by CNN citing North Korean state media, runs alongside the loudspeaker deadline and raises the operational stakes at the border.

Seoul's military says no new North Korean weapons have been spotted at the border, but it is considering proportional measures. South Korea's People Power Party is also urging the government to resume its own loudspeaker broadcasts in response.

A CSIS analysis published today argues North Korea is using calibrated military threats to test the Trump administration's red lines and create conditions for a diplomatic opening on Pyongyang's terms. It also connects Pyongyang's loudspeaker activity and border fortification to a possible summit strategy. Daily N.K. separately reports that Kim Jong Un's domestic schedule has been reduced since October, which some observers read as quiet summit preparation.

The bottom line: loudspeakers on the North Korean side of the border, within days, with troops authorized to act. Seoul's response is still being weighed.

Second, the Bank of Korea holds, but its governor is sending a warning.

The Bank of Korea kept its benchmark interest rate at 3.00 percent at its October meeting, pausing after back-to-back increases as policymakers assessed how prior hikes are flowing through the economy.

Governor Shin Hyun-song used the occasion to issue unusually direct criticism of government spending. He warned that authorities should be cautious about increasing expenditure using temporary revenue sources. That's a public rebuke of expansionary fiscal policy at a moment when the government has been pushing for broader living wage coverage and public sector job creation.

Goldman Sachs assessed that the next rate increase is likely in the first quarter of 2027, not in November.

The hold comes as South Korea's finance minister told CNBC that semiconductor sector growth is expected to continue through next year. South Korea also surpassed Japan in global export rankings, driven by record semiconductor shipments. Early October data showed exports up 105 percent year-on-year, led by chip sales.

On the corporate side, South Korean streaming platform Korai Flix announced plans to go public in the United States through a special purpose acquisition company deal at a 3.5 billion dollar valuation. And neocloud company Elice traded above its initial public offering price on its second day, recovering from a roughly 30 percent drop on its debut.

The rate is held at 3.00 percent, the governor is wary of spending, and the next move isn't expected until 2027.

Third, Seoul is escalating its diplomatic response to the DMZ mine blast.

South Korea plans to send a letter to the president of the U.N. Security Council urging North Korea to take responsibility for the recent demilitarized zone land mine explosion. Seoul will simultaneously appeal to the Security Council over what it calls an armistice violation.

On the ground in Seoul, the Foreign Ministry briefed approximately 40 ambassadors from 16 countries, including the Korea Club and Peace Club diplomatic groups. Director-General Jeong Yeon-du covered the government's North Korea engagement policy, the three-pronged peace coexistence blueprint presented at the U.N. General Assembly, and the findings of a joint South Korean and U.N. Command investigation into the mine explosion. The ministry emphasized the importance of inter-Korean communication to prevent further escalation.

Chinese state media Xinhua reported Seoul's call for North Korea to return to dialogue, framing it in terms consistent with Beijing's preference for negotiation. That report coincided directly with the Foreign Ministry briefing.

Separately, Nikkei reports that South Korea and the United States are in dispute over the transfer of wartime operational control from a U.S. general to a South Korean commander. A top U.S. envoy had already flagged turbulence in the alliance over diverging security and trade priorities. The Quincy Institute argues Seoul's push for the transfer reflects a broader effort to reduce dependence on American command authority.

Seoul is taking the mine blast to the Security Council, briefing allies, and managing alliance friction at the same time.

Now to Pyongyang.

State media led today with Kim Yo Jong's loudspeaker deadline and the border inspection she conducted with marshal Pak Jong Chon. Since that's already the top story, the framing matters: KCNA presented the visit as a leadership decision, not a military one, with Kim personally directing the timeline.

The second top article was Kim Jong Un receiving a reply letter from Chinese President Xi Jinping, in which Xi reaffirmed commitment to strengthening ties with North Korea. Kim has not made a public appearance in two days.

The bottom line from KCNA: border escalation and a China relationship signal in the same news cycle.

The rest of the news, quickly.

North Korean and Russian prosecutors signed a cooperation agreement in Moscow. Russian Prosecutor General Alexander Gutsan and his North Korean counterpart Kim Chol Won signed an updated deal covering joint action on terrorism, smuggling, and cybercrime. That institutionalized legal link between the two countries' prosecution services is now deeper.

South Korea's National Police Agency confirmed it detected a North Korean information technology worker who concealed their identity and secured employment at a domestic private company, performing actual development work. Police are tracing payment flows and investigating potential networks.

The Ministry of Science and ICT raised its civilian cyber crisis alert from caution to warning on October 22, following the discovery of two critical PHP security vulnerabilities allowing remote code execution. That alert level change follows unexplained outages of nearly all North Korean state websites the day before.

The new Yalu River Bridge between North Korea and China is open, but cargo trucks on both sides have been waiting more than three hours due to customs delays.

The Korea Economic Institute of America argues that China and Russia opened new bridges to North Korea a month apart, reflecting strategic competition for Pyongyang's economic alignment. The analysis notes the sequencing gives Pyongyang leverage to play both patrons against each other.

The Independent examines the diplomatic fallout between Seoul and Kyiv over South Korea's withdrawal of some embassy staff from Ukraine and the dispute over captured North Korean prisoners of war. The article says the row involves an alleged secret disclosed at the United Nations and goes beyond the two prisoners at its center.

In the National Assembly, the trade committee's final national audit covered the planned Honam semiconductor cluster, with lawmakers questioning the ministry's timeline and funding. Trade Minister Kim Jeong-gwan said South Korea must join the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership and called for work-week exemptions for strategic industries.

A Gallup Korea poll taken October 13 to 15 put President Lee Jae-myung's approval at 39 percent. The Democratic Party stood at 36 percent, the People Power Party at 27 percent, and independents at 29 percent.

The Supreme Court is scheduled October 27 for a detention warrant review hearing for former first lady Kim Kun-hee, whose current detention expires at the end of October.

Beyond the region.

Unification Minister Chung Dong-young met EU Indo-Pacific Special Envoy Maria Castillo Fernandez on October 22. Chung argued that North Korea returning to international engagement could help end the Russia-Ukraine war. Both sides agreed to continue close communication on peninsula and European security linkages.

Eighteen U.N. Human Rights Council-appointed independent experts issued a joint statement urging China to halt deportations of North Koreans and demanding Pyongyang disclose information on those who have been returned over the past three years.

Finally, the markets. Seoul's main stock index, the KOSPI, fell about 3.5 percent on October 22, closing at about 5,534. The dollar bought about 1,323 won. Brent crude rose to about 112 dollars a barrel, up about 2.75 percent.
