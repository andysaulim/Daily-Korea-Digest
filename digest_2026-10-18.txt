This is the Korea Daily Brief from the CSIS Korea Chair. It's Sunday, October 18.

North Korea just tested an AI-guided torpedo underwater — the sixth weapons event this month alone — while a U.S. general's words about the DMZ have set off a bipartisan firestorm in Seoul over who actually controls Korean territory. Three things today: North Korea's AI torpedo and what Pyongyang's testing surge means, the sovereignty row triggered by the USFK commander's remarks, and South Korea's trade numbers that are rewriting records and raising tariff risk.

North Korea confirmed an underwater test of a new torpedo — one equipped with artificial intelligence guidance and described as high-speed. State media, KCNA, reported the test. That makes the sixth North Korea weapons event in October alone, following tactical ballistic missile tests and submarine-launched cruise missile tests earlier this month.

Kim Jong Un was not publicly confirmed as present at this test. His last confirmed appearance was October 13, five days ago.

The sustained pace is what stands out here. Prime Minister Han Seong-sook said Sunday the government will continue pushing to restart inter-Korean dialogue despite the absence of any North Korea response, adding that Seoul's door remains open. That posture lands on a day when Pyongyang confirmed its sixth test.

Watch for Kim's next public appearance, and for any KCNA reaction to signals about a possible summit.

The sixth weapons event in October — an AI-guided torpedo, confirmed by state media, no Kim appearance for five days.

A comment made by USFK Commander Brunson at a Washington think tank on October 14 has triggered one of the sharpest alliance friction moments in recent memory. Brunson said, and these are his words, that "from UNC's perspective, South Korea begins at its southern fence line" — a formulation that implies the DMZ sits outside South Korean sovereignty.

The reaction in Seoul was immediate and bipartisan. The ruling Democratic Party and the opposition People Power Party both called for Brunson to retract the remark and apologize. South Korea's Ministry of National Defense described the statement as a "personal view" and reaffirmed that Seoul's position on its territorial sovereignty is "firm." UNC separately clarified that DMZ and JSA responsibilities are, in its words, "military and administrative in nature, separate from South Korea sovereignty," adding that "there is no question of South Korea sovereignty."

The editorial went further, asking whether the remark is connected to a deliberate effort to raise the bar for the transfer of wartime operational control — known as OPCON — from U.S. to South Korean command.

The timing adds another layer.

The key question for policy watchers: whether Washington treats this as a miscommunication to be walked back, or whether the DMZ framing reflects a deliberate institutional position.

Bipartisan calls for retraction, the Defense Ministry pushing back, and an editorial connecting the remark to OPCON — that's where this stands.

South Korea's trade surplus with the United States reached 847 billion dollars through September of this year. That figure already far exceeds last year's full-year surplus of 495 billion dollars. The country is tracking toward a first-ever annual surplus above one trillion dollars with the U.S.

At the same time, Korea's long-running trade deficit with China is projected to flip to surplus for the first time since 2022, driven by semiconductor, wireless device, cosmetics, and food exports.

The Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy flags both trends as double-edged. The large U.S. surplus increases South Korea's exposure to Section 301 tariff action. And on the China side, Beijing may respond to a surplus shift with pressure on critical mineral supply.

Finance Minister Lee Hyoung-il told Moody's, Fitch, and S&P in Bangkok on October 16 that Korea is on track for three-percent-range growth in 2026, driven by semiconductor exports and rising capital investment.

An IMF official separately assessed that South Korea is weathering global uncertainties through AI and chip demand.

The record surplus is the headline, but the tariff exposure it creates is the policy problem to watch.

PYONGYANG.

KCNA's coverage today centers entirely on the torpedo test. The agency confirmed an underwater test of a new high-speed, AI-guided precision torpedo. Reuters, the Washington Post, and N.K. News all confirmed the same KCNA report; N.K. News framed it as the latest in what it called a sustained October weapons testing surge.

Kim Jong Un has not made a confirmed public appearance in five days. KCNA carried no other senior official activity today and no reaction to any external developments.

The bottom line from the brief: watch for Kim's next appearance, and for any KCNA response to signals about a possible inter-Korean or U.S.-North Korea meeting.

A few other things worth your attention.

A cybersecurity breach at Korea Electric Power Corporation, KEPCO, turns out to be significantly worse than the company disclosed. A PPP lawmaker revealed through National Assembly data that the October 4 breach exposed facial recognition credentials for 408 employees and passwords for 14,957 CCTV units — neither figure appeared in KEPCO's public statement. The company says it cannot determine how many times the exposed page was accessed.

Separately, the coffee chain Hollys was hacked in a breach running from September through October. Membership data including home addresses was stolen. All members who joined before February 11, 2020 are potentially affected.

On the domestic political front, the Democratic Party is weighing whether to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae over a seven-month Supreme Court vacancy and his refusal to submit related documents to the Assembly. DP floor leader Kim Min-seok said "all methods and avenues" are under discussion. Former Daegu Mayor Hong Joon-pyo of the PPP warned that moving to impeach would put, in his words, "the ruling party on the road to collapse." There's internal DP resistance to the impeachment path as well.

A four-year investigation into the so-called West Sea shooting case ended with no indictment of former President Moon Jae-in or senior officials. The Democratic Party called it vindication and is now demanding a special probe into what it describes as Yoon-era targeting.

President Lee Jae-myung posted on X Sunday to rebuff pressure to end live Cabinet meeting broadcasts. He wrote that ministers and agency heads "speak their minds freely" and that closed sessions "always follow without exception."

In the region: Belarus opened its first-ever embassy in Pyongyang on October 17, following President Lukashenko's inaugural state visit to North Korea earlier this year. Both sides signed a friendship and cooperation treaty, and Minsk flagged active pursuit of North Korea-Russia-Belarus trilateral cooperation.

Nikkei published a photo feature documenting the opening of the new bridge between Sinuiju and Dandong on October 15 — described as China's largest physical connection to the Korean Peninsula in eight decades. Images show trucks crossing in both directions.

The Gallup Korea weekly poll, taken October 13 through 15, showed President Lee Jae-myung's approval up two points to 39 percent. Democratic Party support edged up to 36 percent, People Power Party to 27 percent, and independents fell to 29 percent.

Coming up: the HD Hyundai Heavy Industries union expands its partial strike from October 19 through 23, directly affecting shipbuilding output at a time of record order books.

Markets. Seoul's main stock index closed at 5,668 on Thursday, up about 2 percent. The won strengthened, trading at about 1,330 to the dollar Sunday, up roughly one percent. Brent crude was at about 110 dollars a barrel as of Thursday, up nearly 4 percent. The Bank of Korea's policy rate stands at 2.50 percent following its October decision.
