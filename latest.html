01Top stories
Yonhap / NK News / Reuters, DPRK

Trump floats Kim Jong Un meeting at APEC; Kremlin says Putin would 'of course' meet Kim

Donald Trump told reporters at the White House before departing for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in Shenzhen that he may meet Kim Jong Un, saying Kim's attendance was 'up to President Xi.' The Kremlin separately confirmed Putin would meet Kim 'of course' if he attends.

A South Korean Foreign Ministry official said Seoul and Washington are in close communication on the possibility and that Seoul 'supports dialogue between the United States and North Korea.' Pyongyang has not confirmed any arrangement; North Korea has in recent weeks stated that recognition of its nuclear status is a precondition for engagement.

Yonhap, Japan-Korea

Lee and Takaichi hold fourth bilateral summit in Manila, confirm January Japan visit

Lee Jae-myung and Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi met in Manila on the sidelines of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) summit, their fourth bilateral meeting since Takaichi took office in October last year.

Lee said South Korea-Japan relations have 'brightened more than ever,' citing record-high favorable public sentiment between the two countries in a recent survey, and confirmed he would visit Japan in January as invited. The two leaders agreed to strengthen energy and economic security cooperation given Middle East developments and to deepen strategic communications.

NK News / KCNA via Yonhap, NK-Russia-China, DPRK state media

Kim Jong Un's top aide Jo Yong Won meets Putin in Moscow, pledges support against US 'neo-Nazism'

Jo Yong Won, a Presidium member of the Political Bureau and secretary of the Workers' Party of Korea Central Committee, met Russian President Vladimir Putin at the Kremlin on Monday, vowing to support Moscow's struggle against US 'neocolonialism and neo-Nazism.' The talks took place days before the planned Trump-Xi-Putin summit at APEC in Shenzhen. KCNA also reported Jo met the speaker of Russia's State Duma and addressed a general meeting in Russia.

The U.S.-South Korea Alliance Has Always Depended on Its Managers. Sneider documents multiple friction points in the current alliance: stalled wartime operational control (OPCON) transfer talks, ROK nuclear submarine ambitions, North Korea policy gaps, the Coupang data breach dispute, visa delays for ruling party legislators, and the $350B investment pledge's contested implementation. He cites Ambassador Kang Kyung-wha's own public frustration over severed senior-level dialogue. The piece argues the alliance has survived worse, specifically in the 1970s when Carter threatened troop withdrawals, because experienced diplomats on both sides kept communications open, and warns that such expertise is currently short on the American side. (Daniel Sneider, KEIA)
Revitalizing the U.S.-ROK Alliance: A New Deterrence Framework. The report argues that closer ties among China, Russia, North Korea, and Iran have made the existing US-ROK combined deterrence posture strategically insufficient, requiring both countries to reassign roles and responsibilities. It identifies North Korea's dramatically expanded nuclear threat, China's growing centrality in the authoritarian grouping, and the Russia-North Korea military partnership as the primary drivers for framework revision. The study was funded through the National Defense Research Institute's federally funded research and development center contract. (RAND National Defense Research Institute, RAND)
