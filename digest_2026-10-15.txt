This is the Korea Daily Brief from the CSIS Korea Chair. It's Thursday, October 15.

North Korea fired cruise missiles from a submarine this week — the first underwater launch in nearly three years — and on the same day, a bridge that took a decade to build finally opened between North Korea and China. Meanwhile, South Korea's stock market had one of its worst days in years. Three things today: Pyongyang's submarine missile test, the New Yalu River Bridge opening, and the KOSPI crash.

North Korea test-fired two types of submarine-launched strategic cruise missiles from a submarine in the East Sea on Wednesday. KCNA confirmed the launch Thursday morning. It's the first time Pyongyang has fired missiles from underwater in nearly three years.

This is the fifth North Korean test event in October alone. Earlier this month, Kim Jong Un's government opened the Defense Revolution Museum on October 13th — featuring the Hwasong-20 intercontinental ballistic missile — and on October 12th, North Korea fired a salvo of tactical ballistic missiles. The submarine launches are now on top of all that.

AP released images from the test showing the launches in progress. N.K. Pro's analysis of the Defense Revolution Museum, published today, identifies more than 25 missile launch vehicles and 15 types of drones and naval weapons on display there — and notes the museum may be located at a suspected nuclear site. That open-source catalogue, N.K. Pro argues, is now a significant intelligence reference for allied governments tracking North Korea's strategic systems.

KCNA says Kim Jong Un was not present at the submarine test. He has not made a public appearance in two days. The brief's watch flag is up: whether Kim surfaces publicly — and how state media frames any U.S. diplomatic signal — are the things to track. The bottom line: five test events in a single month, and the first underwater launch in nearly three years.

The same morning North Korea fired those missiles, a bridge opened between North Korea and China.

The New Yalu River Bridge held its opening ceremony at ten in the morning local time Thursday at Sinuiju. Footage from Xinhua showed trucks crossing in both directions. The bridge is the longest crossing on North Korea-China border, and it has been more than a decade in the making — Beijing finished construction long ago, but the opening was repeatedly delayed.

CNN raised the question of whether the bridge will help Pyongyang evade United Nations sanctions. Nikkei reported that cross-border traffic between China and North Korea is expected to increase substantially as a result.

Separately, Chinese travel agents are now openly advertising business delegation trips to Rason and Pyongyang on social media, including factory visits — even though North Korea's tourism ban is technically still in effect.

Russia, meanwhile, pledged at a diplomatic anniversary reception this week to deepen its ties with North Korea. And an N.K. News analysis published today traces how North Korean weapons reached Houthi rebels in Yemen, arguing the Red Sea offensive was partly powered by North Korean-derived arms. A U.S. court also sentenced a Bosnian arms dealer named Sasa Micev to five years in prison for a scheme to procure North Korean anti-aircraft missiles, working with a co-conspirator in New Mexico.

The bridge opening and the missile test together mark a day when North Korea's relationships with its two main partners — China and Russia — and its weapons programs were all visible at once.

South Korea's main stock index had a brutal session Thursday. The KOSPI fell nearly 8 percent to close at 5,554 — a six-month low. It was bad enough that Korea's exchange activated a sell-side sidecar and then triggered a 20-minute circuit breaker as the index kept falling.

Chipmakers led the selloff. Bloomberg reported investors were rushing to unwind exposure to artificial intelligence stocks on concerns about the sustainability of AI demand, combined with U.S. inflation data. Samsung Electronics shares fell more than 9 percent intraday.

The won weakened slightly against the dollar. Brent crude also pulled back. The central bank's policy rate stands at 2.50 percent.

For Seoul's policymakers, the timing is uncomfortable. President Lee Jae-myung met Thursday with the chair of the U.S.-Korea Business Council, Evan Greenberg, and former Speaker Kevin McCarthy, urging them to promote U.S. investment in Korea. Foreign Minister Cho Hyun was delivering a keynote at the same council's 36th annual meeting, saying South Korea is an indispensable partner for U.S. manufacturing and supply chain restructuring in semiconductors, batteries, nuclear energy, and shipbuilding. That coordinated push to reinforce the economic case for the alliance landed on a day when the semiconductor sector was in freefall. A nearly 8 percent drop in the KOSPI is where the market stands tonight.

KCNA ran two major stories today. The first confirmed Wednesday's submarine-launched cruise missile test — two kinds fired from a submarine in the East Sea, the first underwater launch in nearly three years. KCNA's framing treats this as a straightforward military achievement, with no mention of diplomacy. The second KCNA story confirmed the New Yalu River Bridge opening, set for ten in the morning local time Thursday, more than a decade after construction was completed.

The brief's bottom line: KCNA's confirmation of five test events in October, and the first underwater launch in nearly three years, signals the testing surge is continuing even as diplomatic signals about a possible U.S.-North Korea meeting circulate. Watch for how Pyongyang's state media responds to any such signal — and watch for Kim Jong Un's next public appearance after a two-day absence.

A few other items worth your attention this morning.

U.S. federal prosecutors have dropped charges against former CIA analyst Sue Mi Terry, who had been accused of working for South Korean intelligence. No public explanation was given for the decision.

On the alliance relationship: Seoul is pushing back on Washington over the question of wartime operational control. A senior South Korean defense official warned that repeatedly revising the conditions for the transfer would make the process impossible to complete. Defense Minister Kang Shin-chul confirmed Seoul has formally conveyed those concerns to Washington, after U.S. Forces Korea Commander General Xavier Brunson said "conditions have changed." Seoul and Washington have agreed to hold key defense consultations after the U.S. midterm elections. General Brunson, for his part, spoke at the Second Annual Indo-Pacific Summit Thursday and emphasized the alliance has lasted over 70 years.

South Korea and Japan extended their currency swap deal by three years at a meeting in Bangkok. The agreement now runs to November 2029.

South Korea's Democratic Party leader called Thursday for stronger ties with Japan grounded in acknowledgment of history — a signal, the brief notes, of how the ruling party plans to approach expected high-level trilateral engagement.

On personnel: President Lee approved the appointment of Kim Ji-yong as the inaugural director of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency. The National Assembly's Interior Committee adopted the confirmation hearing report by bipartisan agreement, though some members — including within the Democratic Party — registered unqualified opinions over Kim's prosecutorial background.

Approaching his 500th day in office, President Lee's approval rating stands at 44 percent in a poll reported today — up from 37 percent in a Gallup Korea survey taken October 6th through 8th. The Democratic Party's support in that Gallup poll was down, while the People Power Party's was up, with independents at 31 percent.

South Korea's annual Hoguk military field exercise is expected to begin around October 20th. A South Korean microsatellite constellation returned its first Earth images within 24 hours of launch this week.

A CSIS Korea Chair report published today argues that North Korea's military cooperation with Russia has produced an unprecedented expansion of human rights abuses — including troop deployments in active combat — and calls for a coordinated international response linking the Russia axis to the broader human rights accountability framework.

Finally, keep an eye on South Korea's fiscal picture: the government logged its smallest fiscal deficit through August in eight years, according to the budget ministry. Household loans grew at their slowest pace in seven months in September. And Korean Air reported a tenfold jump in third-quarter net profit on strong passenger and cargo demand.

Markets: Seoul's main stock index closed down nearly 8 percent at 5,554, a six-month low, with the circuit breaker triggered mid-session. The won ended at about 1,345 to the dollar, slightly weaker. Brent crude closed at just over 104 dollars a barrel, down about one and a half percent.
