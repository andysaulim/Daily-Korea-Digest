This is the Korea Daily Brief from the CSIS Korea Chair, read by an AI voice. It's Friday, October 30.

Three things today: Kim Jong Un fires missiles and shows off drones; South Korea's spy chief confirms Washington cut intelligence sharing; and a key nuclear deal hits a wall.

North Korea launched around 10 ballistic missiles the same day Kim appeared at a drone weapons show, and Seoul and Washington can't agree on how many missiles flew. U.S. midterm elections are four days away, on November 3, and the G20 Global Financial Stability Conference opens tomorrow.

First, Kim Jong Un spent Thursday at a drone and weapons exhibition, and North Korea fired a salvo of ballistic missiles the same day.

Kim attended the Unmanned Aeronautical Technology Development-2026 expo, where North Korea publicly demonstrated the 600mm multiple rocket launcher it calls the Sniper for the first time. Live-fire demonstrations also included suicide attack drones and tactical cruise missiles. Speaking at the event, Kim said: "The aim to seize the absolute superiority in the drone technology is an indispensable option in the light of the ever-changing patterns of modern warfare. Strength precisely means peace and peace is guaranteed only by strength."

Separately, Seoul's Joint Chiefs of Staff detected around 10 short-range ballistic missiles launched from the Wonsan area toward the East Sea. The U.S. assessment, however, counted only 2 ballistic missile launches. That discrepancy is unresolved.

South Korea's National Security Office said the launches violated U.N. Security Council resolutions. Trilateral director-general consultations among Seoul, Washington, and Tokyo were held by phone today, October 30.

The National Bureau of Asian Research has a new paper surveying elite opinion in Washington, Tokyo, and Seoul on the durability of the trilateral framework. It finds divergent threat perceptions and domestic pressures in each capital as the primary strains on Camp David commitments, and it frames those findings directly against this week's provocations.

The first-public Sniper rocket launcher, around 10 missiles from Wonsan, and a contested count between allies: that's the picture from the Korean Peninsula today.

Second, South Korea's intelligence chief has confirmed what had been reported but not officially acknowledged, the United States restricted intelligence sharing with Seoul.

National Intelligence Service Director Lee Jong-seok told the National Assembly Intelligence Committee on October 29 that Washington began limiting what it shared after Unification Minister Chung Dong-young publicly disclosed information about a suspected North Korean nuclear facility earlier this year.

Lee also told the committee that U.S. visa delays affecting National Intelligence Service personnel assigned to the U.S. Embassy have been resolved, and that those staff are working normally. The service characterized the delays as a tightening of global U.S. entry policy, not a bilateral dispute.

The intelligence-sharing restriction adds a new dimension to an already strained moment in the alliance. U.S. and South Korean defense chiefs agreed on October 28 to delay wartime operational control transfer talks to early next year. Presidential National Security Adviser Wi Sung-lac said today that the intelligence restrictions need to be resolved and that he expects substantive progress on wartime operational control at the Security Consultative Meeting scheduled for December.

Bloomberg this week examined constraints on Seoul's defense buildup, arguing the operational control delay and alliance tensions amount to a reality check on South Korea's military ambitions.

The bottom line: a confirmed intelligence cutoff, a delayed operational control timeline, and a December meeting that Seoul is now treating as the next pressure point.

Third, a major South Korean nuclear project is in trouble.

Korea Hydro and Nuclear Power terminated negotiations with Daewoo Engineering and Construction and Doosan Enerbility over their subcontracting role in the 18.7 billion dollar Czech Dukovany nuclear plant project. The talks collapsed over cost disputes. Korea Hydro and Nuclear Power says the overall project timeline will not be delayed but has not named replacement contractors.

The breakdown comes as Japan's IHI is actively competing for overseas nuclear demand in the same European market, putting pressure on Seoul to resolve the contractor gap quickly.

The Korean government had separate nuclear energy business this week. South Korea hosted the International Atomic Energy Agency's International Ministerial Conference on Nuclear Power in Incheon, which closed with small modular reactors and nuclear-AI integration on the agenda. A U.S. State Department official, Deputy Assistant Secretary Christopher Klein, said on the margins of that conference that the U.S. and South Korea can achieve "great things" in small modular reactors, calling the moment a "nuclear renaissance."

The 18.7 billion dollar Czech contract, no replacement contractors named: that's where things stand.

Now to Pyongyang.

State media led today with Kim's drone-supremacy call. KCNA covered the Unmanned Aeronautical Technology Development-2026 exhibition, including the first public firing of the 600mm Sniper launcher, that's the same story that opened this broadcast. State media framed it as a demonstration of Kim's vision for AI-enabled warfare.

KCNA also ran an article slamming South Korea-U.S. Freedom Flag air defense drills, vowing that Pyongyang will counter what it calls hostile military activity with force.

One other item from Pyongyang today: Swiss Foreign Minister Ignazio Cassis attended the opening of Switzerland's first diplomatic office in Pyongyang, the most significant Western re-engagement signal in the North Korean capital in years.

The rest of the news, quickly.

Prosecutors have asked for the death penalty for former President Yoon in his ongoing criminal trial over the martial law declaration. The sentencing request has been filed.

On the alliance, U.S. Forces Korea Commander General Xavier Brunson called the Multi-Domain Task Force "absolutely attractive" for Korea, a pitch that has raised concerns about China contingency planning. General Brunson also walked the Joint Security Area with German Chancellor Friedrich Merz today.

Defense Minister Kang Shin-chul attended the 13th ADMM-Plus in the Philippines on October 29, his first multilateral security forum since taking office. He held separate bilateral talks with Singapore and Vietnam on the same day.

On South Korea's reported diplomatic tilt toward Russia: multiple Ukrainian outlets cited a Financial Times report on Seoul moving closer to Moscow as ties with Kyiv deteriorate. One Ukrainian outlet reported Seoul had not ruled out severing diplomatic ties with Kyiv, though the South Korean government has not confirmed that characterization. Republican Congressman Joe Wilson separately condemned what he called Seoul's dangerous and insulting tilt toward Russia.

On the economy: South Korea's industrial output in September rose 3.0 percent, the largest gain in 15 and a half years, with consumer spending and investment rising at the same time.

Seoul also unveiled its first retirement pension overhaul in 21 years. The plan would shift to a fund-based system with contributions pooled from multiple companies under professional management, requiring all companies to deposit workers' retirement benefits with external institutions by 2030.

Gallup Korea's latest poll, taken October 30, finds the Democratic Party leads the People Power Party by 5 percentage points, 35 percent to 30 percent, the narrowest gap since the current government took office.

On the special counsel front: the team investigating ballot shortages during the June 3 election in Seoul's Songpa Ward asked the National Election Commission to preserve 420 uninspected ballot boxes and transferred 13 boxes for forensic examination.

A blockchain intelligence firm has linked North Korea to a 6.3 billion dollar hack of Binance, described as the largest cryptocurrency theft on record, surpassing the 2025 Bybit breach.

On Capitol Hill.

U.S. lawmakers are advancing legislation that would ease the transfer of American-origin munitions to Ukraine through South Korea and allied nations. Separately, Republican Congressman Joe Wilson publicly condemned Seoul's reported tilt toward Russia, calling it dangerous and insulting to alliance principles. U.S. midterm elections are November 3, which will shape the fiscal year 2027 defense appropriations outlook and Korea-related legislation.
